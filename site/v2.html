V2 results

V2 rebuilt the score after V1's open-source timer turned out to miss whole classes of circuit paths. Every design is now timed by the FPGA vendor's own tool, with memory stalls present in the timed circuit, and ranked on five hidden programs the agents never see. Six agent systems (a model inside its vendor's command-line agent) each ran six times from the same starting core, 15 rounds of 3 attempts per run.

Result Three tiers: Opus 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5 (Claude Code), then GPT-6.1 Sol and GPT-6 Astra (Codex CLI), then GPT-5.5 and GPT-6 Luna (Codex CLI). Pre-registered test, Opus 5.5 vs GPT-6.1 Sol: 1.32x, 95% CI 1.18 to 1.47, p = 0.0005.

Baselines and controls

What the agents beat

Textbook edit
V0's single-cycle divider replaced by a radix-2 iterative one, the first move most agents make (34 of the 35 designs accepted in round 1 change the divider). It passes every gate and scores 2,645 held-out iter/s, 8.6x V0, all of it clock frequency. Every scored run beats it; system means are 1.3x (GPT-6 Luna) to 2.7x (Opus 5.5) its score.
Random-mutation control
No model: 1 to 3 random single-line edits per attempt (swapped operators, changed constants), 135 attempts over 3 runs. 0 accepted: 117 fail formal verification, 12 fail co-simulation against an instruction-set simulator, 6 pass every gate but change nothing the design does. The gates do not hand out improvements to blind edits.
Lessons ablation
GPT-6.1 Sol run six more times without the lessons file (one-line notes an agent writes after each attempt for later rounds). Full vs no lessons: 1.02x, 95% CI 0.91 to 1.14, p = 0.68. Not distinguishable; the interval rules out a gain from the lessons above about 14%.
Human reference cores
Ten open-source RV32IM cores built on the same flow with the same stall model. VexRiscv MaxPerf (6,216) falls between the first and second tiers; the other nine score below every second-tier system.
Robustness

Does the ranking hold?

Place-and-route settings. All final designs rebuilt under the six Gowin placement and routing settings that change a build: the same system order, Opus 5.5 vs GPT-6.1 Sol between 1.28x and 1.33x (every p < 0.001), and the same 9 of 12 separating pairs under every setting.

Unseen programs. On 15 more Embench-IoT programs no agent saw, the system order, the rank intervals and the three indistinguishable pairs are the same; all 740 design-program runs give correct results.

Another FPGA. Every design synthesized for an AMD Artix-7 with Vivado. The split between the top four and the bottom two holds; the order inside the top four does not (Opus 5.5 vs GPT-6.1 Sol: 1.03x, p = 0.80). The agents' designs gain less from the faster fabric than the human cores (Fmax ratio 1.17 vs 1.67), which suggests that fifteen rounds of tuning against one vendor's timing report fit a design to that part. The V2 ranking is a ranking on the Gowin contract.

Correctness

What the gates missed

Three gaps in the correctness checks surfaced during and after scoring; none changes a scored result.

  • Instruction fetch address. No gate proves that the fetch address stays inside memory. A bounded formal check written after scoring (any 64-word program whose own branches stay in range, arbitrary memory stalls, 20 cycles from reset) passes 36 of 44 designs and finds one failure: an Opus 5.5 design fetches from below address 0 on a mispredicted path, 9 cycles after reset. Its results stay correct. Seven larger designs time out without a counterexample.
  • Multiply and divide arithmetic. The per-round formal gate replaces multiplier and divider results with stand-in formulas, so co-simulation checks the arithmetic. The vendored formal specification for signed DIV and REM computes the unsigned result, so the contract's full-arithmetic check fails every correct divider.
  • Formal saw different code. Two final designs gave the formal tool a different version of one block than synthesis and simulation got. Both pass all 53 checks when formal sees the synthesized version.
